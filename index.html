The Problem

The work started before the work.

It started with one student: a 15-year-old visual thinker who could plan complex builds, but kept getting blocked by the way school assignments arrived.

Assignments came home as dense blocks of language: what to do, how to do it, when it was due, where to submit it, what counted, and the steps he was expected to infer.

Before he could start the actual assignment, he had to turn that wall of text into a plan. He had to find the steps, put them in order, remember what mattered, and hold all of it in his head.

That invisible first task was draining the energy needed for the real one.

Recognition

The opening was already there.

I recognized the shape of that struggle because I think visually too. I know what it feels like to understand something internally, but spend too much energy translating the way it was handed to me before I could begin.

The breakthrough came from a Lego project.

After a conversation about a graduation gift, the student sent over a detailed vision board at 7:00 AM: images, sequence, parts, and a clear build plan. No one had forced that structure onto him. He made it because the format made sense.

That changed the question.

Instead of asking how to push him through another wall of instructions, I started asking how schoolwork could be translated into a form he already knew how to move through.

A build.

The first version of The Build Board used his own language and interests — Lego, Minecraft, and aviation — to turn assignments into visual pieces. Math became pre-flight checklists. Homework became simulator training. The student stayed in the pilot seat.

This was a fundamental mismatch in information architecture: the work was being delivered one way, and the student processed information another way.

The Build Board did not change the assignment. It changed the shape of the information around it.

How It Works

Capture, translate, build.

Three steps, one consistent flow. The same process works for every subject — math sets, lab reports, reading responses, essays.

Build

Build it, piece by piece

See the prompt that builds the board open / close

Plain-language, no jargon. The same instructions produce the same kind of board for any assignment.

Goal: Turn a school assignment into a clear, modular build plan.

Rewrite the assignment below as a "Build Board":
- Break it into the smallest useful steps. One action per step.
- Label each step "Piece 1, Piece 2…" with a short instruction
  a 13-year-old can act on without re-reading.
- Start each piece with a verb (Find, Write, Build, Check, Upload).
- Keep the rigor. Don't cut requirements — just sequence them.
- End with a "Finished Build" line describing what done looks like.
- Note the due date and where it gets submitted.

Reflection

What I learned from building it.

The work I'm proudest of usually starts the same way — by watching closely where someone gets stuck, before reaching for a tool.

With this student, the hard part lived in the space between reading an assignment and knowing where to begin.

So the work was about making that path visible: small enough to start, clear enough to follow, without changing what the assignment actually asked for.

That's the thread I keep coming back to — find the friction someone's quietly working around, make it visible, and build something simple enough to actually use.

Where It Could Go

From one board to a larger system.

The Build Board started with one student, but the same approach reaches further. High-schoolers are going to encounter AI regardless. The opening is in giving them a structured way to use it well, rather than leaving them to figure it out at random.

Early on, I can see it growing into a student-controlled learning interface — one that reads assignment context, a learning profile, the subject, and a student's skill goals, then generates the right kind of scaffold for the moment:

  • Sequencing

    Visual board

    For breaking a multi-step assignment into ordered pieces.

  • Math

    Pre-flight checklist

    For running a rule or procedure the same way every time.

  • Comprehension

    Reading map

    For finding structure and meaning inside a dense text.

  • Essays

    Writing frame

    For generating ideas, then shaping them into an argument.

  • Progress

    Flight log

    For tracking what's done and what's been practiced over time.

The goal is to help students see the path, practice the skill, and build confidence —

with tools they'll need in school, work, and life.
